\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This study evaluates the impact of climate-related risks on the Loss Given Default (LGD) of a residential mortgage loan portfolio, through a climate stress-testing exercise based on French data and NGFS scenario projections. A dual methodological framework is implemented, combining a Top-Down approach---linking LGD to macroeconomic deterioration under NGFS climate scenarios---and a Bottom-Up approach capturing the direct effects of physical and transition risks on collateral values.

In the Top-Down analysis, climate scenarios influence a latent systemic factor---calibrated from macroeconomic indicators---which jointly drives the Probability of Default (PD) and LGD. Several PD--LGD dependency models are explored to characterize this relationship under stress. Among them, the structural Merton model is selected for its analytical consistency and better fit. Results show that delayed policy responses lead to the most significant rise in projected LGDs, while gradual transitions induce milder effects.

The Bottom-Up approach complements this by assessing the direct financial impact of flood exposure and property devaluation linked to energy inefficiency. These shocks are reflected in stressed Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratios---defined as the ratio between the outstanding loan amount and the current collateral value---which are then used to estimate LGD. Results show a consistent directional response: more adverse climate scenarios are associated with higher LGD levels, though the magnitude remains moderate due to data limitations and portfolio features.

Overall, this study offers a solid starting point for understanding the mechanisms and modelling approaches required to conduct climate stress tests on credit risk parameters---particularly LGD. It also underscores several empirical and methodological challenges that must be addressed to improve credit risk modelling in the context of climate change.

\chapter*{Glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Glossary}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
    \item \textbf{Bottom-Up Approach}: A risk modelling technique that starts at the individual loan or asset level and aggregates results to obtain a portfolio-wide risk assessment.
    \item \textbf{Climate Risk}: The potential financial losses and credit risks arising from physical climate events and transition risks in banking and finance.
    \item \textbf{EAD (Exposure at Default)}: The total value that a bank is exposed to when a borrower defaults on a loan, representing the outstanding amount at the time of default.
    \item \textbf{Forward Looking}: A risk assessment approach that incorporates future expectations and projected macroeconomic scenarios rather than relying solely on historical data.
    \item \textbf{LGD (Loss Given Default)}: The proportion of a loan or credit exposure that is lost when the borrower defaults.
    \item \textbf{NGFS (Network for Greening the Financial System)}: A global network of central banks and financial supervisors that aims to enhance the financial sector’s role in achieving sustainability and managing climate-related risks.
    \item \textbf{PD (Probability of Default)}: The probability that a borrower or counterparty will default on its payment obligations within a given time horizon.
    \item \textbf{Physical Risk}: The risk of direct financial losses due to extreme weather events (e.g., hurricanes, floods) or long-term environmental changes (e.g., rising sea levels).
    \item \textbf{PIT (Point In Time)}: A credit risk measurement approach that reflects current economic conditions and a borrower’s short-term risk.
    \item \textbf{Stress Testing}: A technique used to evaluate how a financial institution or system would perform under extreme adverse conditions, helping to assess vulnerabilities.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Factor}: A macroeconomic or financial variable that affects credit risk parameters (such as PD, LGD) across multiple borrowers simultaneously.
    \item \textbf{Top-Down Approach}: A risk modelling technique where macroeconomic variables drive portfolio-wide risk estimations, typically used by regulators and central banks.
    \item \textbf{Transition Risk}: Financial risks arising from the shift to a low-carbon economy, including regulatory changes, market shifts, and reputational impacts.
    \item \textbf{TTC (Through The Cycle)}: An approach that estimates credit risk parameters (such as PD, LGD) by smoothing out short-term fluctuations in economic conditions, making it more stable.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

In the realm of credit risk, two principal metrics are used to assess a portfolio: the \textit{Probability of Default (PD)} and the \textit{Loss Given Default (LGD)} defined in \textcite{bis_2005}. PD is the likelihood of a borrower failing to meet their obligations (i.e., defaulting) within a specified timeframe, whereas LGD quantifies the severity of losses that lenders or investors may incur when defaults occur. To assess the capital requirements that best correspond to their exposure to adverse risks, financial institutions have developed their own internal methodologies to stress these parameters.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Moreover, given the increasing materialization of climate risks—through physical risks (e.g., extreme weather events) and transition risks (e.g., regulatory shifts toward a low-carbon economy)—it has become essential to integrate climate risk into credit risk modelling. Thus, understanding the relationship between credit risk metrics and climate conditions is now a cornerstone of stress testing, commonly referred to as climate stress testing, as mentioned in \textcite{ESRB_2016}. For this purpose, the European Central Bank (ECB) carried out a climate stress-testing exercise in 2021. In this exercice, two main transmission channels through which climate risks impact credit risk were identified : the Top-Down (or indirect) approach and the Bottom-Up (or direct) approach (see \textcite{alogoskoufis2021ecb}).

\vspace{0.3cm}

In the Top-Down approach, PDs are stressed based on the impact of climate-threatening scenarios on economic conditions, a process known as Point-in-Time (PiT) modelling. Since there are numerous data sources and methodologies available, this aspect is widely applied. However, a key limitation remains overlooked: LGD is often assumed to be constant and conservative, following the Through-The-Cycle (TTC) modelling approach. As a result, during a stress test exercise, the sensitivity of capital requirements to various scenarios is typically captured by PDs alone, without properly accounting for LGD variations. \textcite{cirillo_modeling_2018} have shown in their paper that this static assumption can lead to an underestimation of potential losses, particularly in the context of climate stress testing, where financial shocks can significantly alter recovery rates and collateral values.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Moreover, the Basel II Framework in \textcite{basel2005guidance} states that the LGD parameter used in Pillar 1 capital calculations must reflect economic downturn conditions where necessary to capture the relevant risks. Therefore, to model LGD accurately, it is crucial to consider its dependence on economic factors. Thus, in order to incorporate the LGD dependence on economic conditions, in the top-down approach, many authors have proposed modelling the PD-LGD dependency structure. This approach acknowledges that economic downturns simultaneously increase default rates and reduce recoveries (wrong-way risk), leading to a reinforcing effect on credit losses. By incorporating this dependency into climate stress testing, we can better capture the full extent of credit risk exposure under different climate scenarios.

\vspace{0.4cm}

In the Bottom-Up approach, the focus is on examining the direct impact of climate-related factors on asset values and collateral quality. This method involves estimating how physical and transition risks affect specific industries, borrowers, and collateralized assets. Since these risks can alter the liquidation value of assets, they ultimately influence PD and LGD calculations.

\vspace{0.3cm}


In this report, we aim to conduct a climate stress test specifically on the LGD parameter, assessing how climate-driven economic shocks and asset devaluations influence loss severities in a portfolio of residential mortgage loans in France. To achieve this goal, we will use a dataset of defaulted mortgage loans, along with historical macroeconomic data for France and projections up to 2050 under different NGFS climate scenarios.


\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Climate risks}

Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges the world faces in this century, and governments worldwide have committed to addressing it in various ways. The Paris \textcite{agreement2015united} marked a significant milestone in this regard, as it aims to foster a global response to climate change by keeping the global temperature rise this century well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase even further to 1.5°C.\\

This issue has filled the financial world, as it represents not only an economic risk but also a regulatory and reputational one. Indeed, many European action plans and national legislations (e.g., taxonomy, disclosure requirements, etc.) have been developed for sustainable finance. This has led to a so-called “regulatory avalanche”, with which banks must comply. 

Climate change gives rise to two main categories of climate-related risk: \textit{physical risk} and \textit{transition risk} (see \textcite{semieniuk2021low} and \textcite{alogoskoufis2021ecb})
Transition risk refers to the negative impact that climate policies designed to reduce $CO_2$ emissions could have on high-emitting firms. For instance, industries heavily reliant on non-renewable or highly polluting resources—such as mining or fossil fuel extraction—could experience a sharp decline in profits and rising production costs. Hence, failing to meet expectations in terms of pollution poses a significant global reputational risk to both the bank and its business.
Another key driver of climate-related risk is physical risk, which stems from the increasing frequency and severity of natural disasters due to climate change. For example, production facilities located in high-risk areas—such as coastal regions prone to flooding—are particularly vulnerable. In the event of extreme climate events, these facilities could sustain substantial damage, leading to short-term production disruptions and, in the long run, potential business failures.\\

These two categories of climate risk constitute an emerging source of systemic risk, with the potential to disrupt financial institutions ability to provide services and threaten the stability of financial markets, ultimately impacting the real economy. First, transition risk could undermine the creditworthiness of banks’ counterparties and lead to declines in asset prices, potentially endangering bank solvency. Moreover, physical risk could lead to sudden financial losses in climate-sensitive geographical areas, eroding collateral values and reducing the worth of assets held by financial institutions.


\section{Integrating climate risk into LGD modelling}

% In recent years, central banks and regulatory institutions have begun developing stress-testing methodologies and scenario analyses to assess the impact of climate risks on the financial system and the broader economy. While significant progress has been made, most of these frameworks still lack certain critical components needed for a comprehensive evaluation of climate risks on financial stability and economic resilience.\\

In 2021, the ECB developed an economy-wide climate stress test to assess the resilience of non-financial corporations and euro area banks to climate risks under various assumptions regarding future climate policies. This paper serves as a key reference in term of climate stress-testing, as the ECB plays a central role in the banking sector. Moreover, this study centralised many others studies on the subject (see ACPR and Banque de France (2020); ACPR and Banque de France (2021); Banque de France (2020); EBA (2021))
It highlights that climatic factors can induce financial and banking risks through several transmission channels, which can be grouped into two main categories: direct and indirect transmission channels of climate risk drivers. These channels are commonly referred to as the bottom-up and top-down approaches, respectively.\\

To integrate climate risk into LGD modelling, a common approach is to stress-test the PD parameter through a systemic factor Z, assuming it is linked to macroeconomic factors. The impact of this stressed systemic factor on LGD is then observed using some of the PD-LGD dependence models discussed in the previous section. This method is known as the Top-Down approach.
For the modelling of the relationship between macroeconomic conditions (captured through the systemic factor 
Z) and Point-in-Time (PIT) PDs, the \textit{Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF)} model of \textcite{vasicek2002distribution} serves as the reference framework . These stressed PIT PDs are then linked to LGD through the different PD-LGD dependence specifications proposed in the section \ref{pd-lgd-literature}.

A phase of translating climate scenarios into economic consequences is then necessary, in order to stress PD and LGD. In the literature, models such as the Integrated Assessment Model (IAMs) and the National Institute Global Econometric Model (NiGEM), from the Network of Central Banks and Supervisors for Greening the Financial system (NGFS), are used for this purpose (see \textcite{alogoskoufis2021ecb}, \textcite{aboh2023developpement},...). The input variables of these models concern forecasts of climate shocks (future trajectory of global temperature, CO2 emissions, carbon price, etc.). \\

Another approach is the Bottom-Up approach, where the impact of climate risk on LGD is modelled directly. Although there are not many studies on this approach, a few models have been developed.
To assess the impact of physical risk - particularly flooding- on LGD, the most used model is the LISFLOOD hydrodynamic model of \textcite{dottori2016development}. This model generates water height maps associated with river floods of varying intensities, characterized by return periods, across all European countries.
These maps provide essential inputs for calculating expected damages to properties, which in turn affect the Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio—a key driver of LGD. Moreover, the Expected Annual Damage methodology of \textcite{huizinga2017global}, recommended by the ECB, is employed to quantify flood impacts on asset values by integrating probability-weighted damage functions. 

In addition to physical risks, transition risks also significantly affect asset values and, consequently, the LGD. To assess the impact of transition risk on LGD, it is common to model the expected devaluation of properties with poor energy performance certificates (EPCs - Energy Performance Certificates /GHG - GreenHouse Gas labels) through updated Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratios. Several studies, including \textcite{roncalli2022handbook}, have demonstrated how integrating energy performance ratings into real estate pricing models helps capture the expected depreciation under transition scenarios.
Moreover, to estimate the expected devaluation of energy-inefficient properties, \textcite{ter2021german} proposed a model, based on the assumption that less energy-efficient properties will, all other things being equal, be sold at lower prices than more energy-efficient homes. 


\section{Dependence between PD and LGD : towards a joint modelling approach}\label{pd-lgd-literature}

In the modelling of Loss Given Default (LGD), the integration of economic conditions in the calculation of the LGD Point in Time, via the Forward Looking concept, is sometimes neglected. This represents a major challenge in credit risk assessment due to the complexity of its dynamics and its interdependence with several economic factors. One of the main difficulties lies in the fact that LGD is not a constant but varies with the probability of default (PD) and, hence, economic conditions, as mentioned in \textcite{altman2005link}. Quantifying this relationship becomes even more complex due to the limited availability of LGD data, making the development of robust and accurate models particularly difficult. This uncertainty, combined with the lack of complete data and the variability of factors influencing losses, complicates the modelling process significantly.
Hence, to obtain a stressed LGD, a common approach is to stress test PD and then observe its impact on LGD through their dependency relationship. Below, we review different methods for modelling this relationship.\\

% \textcolor{orange}{Charles Emmanuel : La LGD workout n'est pas une méthode d'estimation de la LGD, c'est une méthode de calcul, i.e. la manière dont la variable cible y=LGD est construite. Parler de la LGD workout dans cette section semble donc peu approprié.}
% \textbf{Commentaire : Le passage sur LGD Workout a été supprimé car il ne correspond pas à une méthode de modélisation mais seulement à un mode de calcul.}

Various approaches have been proposed in the literature to model the PD-LGD relationship. Works such as those of \textcite{frye2013credit}, \textcite{pykhtin2003recovery}, and \textcite{cirillo_modeling_2018} have demonstrated that this systemic dependence significantly impacts the overall risk estimation of a portfolio. Frye proposes a linear modelling of LGD dependent on a latent systematic factor, though this factor is rarely observable in practice. Pykhtin extends this approach by introducing models that require an in-depth knowledge of the systematic factor $ Y $, which, while theoretically relevant, poses challenges due to its latent nature.\\

A more flexible solution is proposed by \textcite{giese2005impact}, who introduces a nonlinear model incorporating a more adaptable link function between LGD and PD. This model accounts for economic dynamics but assumes a comonotone relationship \footnote{A comonotone relationship refers to a relationship between two variables where they always move in the same direction, reflecting maximum dependence driven by a common factor.}, which may lead to an overestimation of large quantiles in the loss distribution, especially during crises. Moreover, the proposed link function lacks theoretical justification from both an economic and a statistical perspective, which may limit its applicability in real-world scenarios.\\

To better capture the relationship between PD and LGD, \textcite{cirillo_modeling_2018} propose a bivariate non-central Beta distribution, which models their joint structure and enhances regulatory capital estimates. The dependence is modelled using a common factor $W$, enabling simultaneous stress testing of both parameters. However, accurate calibration remains a significant challenge, requiring representative historical data across different economic cycles.\\

\textcite{hillebrand2006modeling} take a different approach by incorporating stochastic dependence rather than deterministic functional relationships. He argues that expected LGD and PD, conditioned on economic cycles, are not necessarily comonotone but exhibit stochastic dependency, making the model more realistic under varying market conditions.\\

Within a structural modelling framework, \textcite{tasche2004single} assumes that LGD depends on the logarithmic return of an asset falling below the debt value. Similarly, the Merton-based model proposed by \textcite{seidler2009implied} links LGD to asset volatility, leveraging credit risk theory to estimate recoveries. However, these approaches require precise asset volatility estimation, which can be complex and highly sensitive to market fluctuations.

Building on the idea of linking credit risk components, \textcite{frye2013credit} propose a model where PD and LGD evolve in tandem with economic fluctuations. Their approach assumes that the default rate (DR) and the loss rate (LR) are perfectly linked, following a Vasicek distribution, with a single correlation parameter governing their dependency. While computationally efficient, this model's reliance on a fixed correlation structure may limit its flexibility.

A similar correlation-based perspective is adopted in the Vasicek model introduced by \textcite{li1999default} employs a Gaussian copula approach to link PD and LGD via a shared systemic factor representing macroeconomic conditions. This model is particularly relevant for regulatory stress testing as it effectively captures macroeconomic dependencies. However, its implementation requires extensive data calibration and is computationally demanding.
In the end, each model presents distinct advantages and challenges. The Frye-Jacobs model is easy to implement but assumes a rigid correlation structure. The Merton-based model is dynamic but relies heavily on market data. The Vasicek model effectively captures macroeconomic dependencies but requires complex calibration.\\

Based on this literature review, we have selected the Hillebrand, Frye-Jacobs, and structural Merton-based models to capture PD-LGD dependency and, consequently, implement the Top-Down approach. This choice is founded on the applicability of these models and their distinct theoretical foundations. Specifically, Hillebrand offers a statistical perspective, whereas the Merton-based model adopts a structural approach, while Frye-Jacobs provides an economic cycle-based framework. Together, these models offer a comprehensive view of PD-LGD dependencies, ensuring robustness in stress testing and risk quantification.
For the Bottom-Up approach, where established methodologies are limited, we will test all the models identified in this review.

\section{Description of the portfolio}

The portfolio used to assess the integration of climate risk into LGD estimation consists of mortgage loans granted to individuals and professionals who defaulted between 2010 and 2019. The dataset combines three key sources:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mortgage loan database: Contains detailed data on defaulting borrowers (creditworthiness, financial history, property address, LGD) as well as information on the financed real estate. Note that the property address and its corresponding latitude and longitude do not necessarily reflect the building’s exact location but rather the nearest address with similar characteristics, as determined by the geocoder developed by Square Management's teams.
    
    \item National Building Database (BDNB): Aggregates several Open Data sources (BD TOPO, DLE, ETALAB) and provides comprehensive information on the characteristics of real estate properties in France.
    
    \item Property Transaction Database (DVF): A French public database that records real estate transactions registered by notaries and tax services.
\end{itemize}

These additional databases (BDNB and DVF) enrich the banking data by supplying complementary information on the properties subject to mortgage loans. A complete list of variables is provided in table~\ref{tab:data_variables} in the appendix.

\subsection{Loan and borrower characteristics}

The dataset includes detailed financial and risk-related attributes for each borrower and mortgage contract, such as the loan amount, the risk score of the individuals at the time of the loans, the loss given default (LGD), the exposure at default (EAD) etc. Thus, these informations are important for developing a Basel-compliant LGD model that relies on Exposure at Default (EAD) and recovery values. 

The portfolio consists of two types of clients: individual and professional clients, both covered by mortgage and external guarantee guarantees, each with distinct risk characteristics. In fact, the overall portfolio has a realised LGD of 20.30\%, associated with a risk score of 18.99\%. Therefore, the probability that the portfolio will default is not high, and the loss incurred in the event of default amounts to only 20\% of an exposed amount of approximately 115 092 euros. However, this loss is not homogeneous across client types. Professional clients tend to have a lower probability of default (7.81\%) but a higher exposure amount, whereas individuals exhibit the opposite trend, with a default probability of 19.06\% but a lower EAD. Regarding guarantee types, loans secured by mortgage guarantees have the highest risk exposure.

To compute the realized LGD (commonly referred to as LGD workout) for a contract $j$ that has defaulted during a specific period, and for which the recovery process has been completed, we apply the following formula:
\begin{align*}
     LGD_j &= 1 - \frac{\mbox{Present Value of the contract recovery cash flows for contract } j}{EAD_j} \\
     &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=\tau_j}^T \mbox{Discounted Recoveries}_{jt}}{EAD_{j,\tau_j}} \\
     &= 1 - TR_j(T_j),
\end{align*}
\hspace{0.65cm}  
where $TR_j(T_j)$ denotes the final recovery rate of contract $j$. Recoveries are observed from the default date $\tau_j$ until the resolution of the default $T_j$.

In our dataset, some LGD values exceed 1 (indicating that the recovery process incurred higher costs than the exposure). For consistency in our analyses, we clip LGD values at 1.

\subsection{Property characteristics and energy labels}

The dataset includes information on the underlying real estate assets, particularly their energy efficiency.
EPCs and GHG labels are used in real estate diagnostics to inform about a property's energy performance and environmental impact.
EPCs measures a dwelling's or building's energy consumption in kilowatt-hours per square meter per year(kWh/m\textsuperscript{2}/year).
Housing is classified on a scale from A (very efficient) to G (very energy-intensive). 

The GHG label, on the other hand, measures $\mbox{CO}_2$ (carbon dioxide) emissions generated by the real estate property, expressed in kg of $\mbox{CO}_2$/m\textsuperscript{2}/year \footnote{In practice, each energy source used in the dwelling is converted into kg of $\mbox{CO}_2$/m\textsuperscript{2}/year using an emission factor specific to each energy type. 
For example, in France, electricity has a low emission thanks to nuclear power ($\approx 0.09$ kg $\mbox{CO}_2$/kWh), while natural gas emits around $0.234$ kg $\mbox{CO}_2$/kWh, and domestic fuel oil $\approx 0.324$ kg $\mbox{CO}_2$/kWh.}.
EPCs and GHG labels thus provide complementary insights. Generally, a high EPCs label also implies a high GHG label, but some exceptions exist.
For instance, consider a well-insulated dwelling heated with natural gas. It will receive a good EPCs rating due to its efficient insulation and optimized energy use. However, it will have a poor GHG label since natural gas is a fossil fuel that produces a significant amount of $\mbox{CO}_2$ upon combustion. Our portfolio consists primarily of mortgages with Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions labels of B and Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) labels of D (see table \ref{tab:epc_ghg_label}).

\section{NGFS Climate Scenarios}

The Network of Central Banks and Supervisors for Greening the Financial System ( Network for Greening the Financial System-NGFS) has developed future climate scenarios on the basis of historical trends, but also on the potential amplitude risks that could arise in the future. At the current version of these scenarios is shown in figure \ref{fig:ngfs_cart} :

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/ngfs_scenario.png}
    \caption{Cartography of NGFS Scenario (\href{https://www.ngfs.net/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/ngfs-climate-scenarios-central-banks-and-supervisors-0}{Source : NGFS phase V, 2024})}
    \label{fig:ngfs_cart}
\end{figure}

According to NGFS, the definition of climate scenarios aims to provide a common reference framework to understand the future evolution of climate change risks, both physical risks and transition risks arising from energy transition policies and technologies. These scenarios are designed by highlighting either their pessimistic (high-risk) or optimistic (low-risk) nature through an analysis of physical and transition risks. They are described as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordered Scenarios} : These scenarios assume that climate policies are implemented very early and gradually become stricter. As a result, the physical and transition risks incurred are lower. By introducing climate policies quickly enough to reduce $CO_2$ emissions and lower global temperatures, these scenarios translate into moderate physical and transition risks. They are divided into three alternative scenarios : \textit{Net Zero 2050}, \textit{Below 2°C}, and \textit{Low Demand}.
    
    The \textit{Net Zero 2050} scenario aims to limit global warming to 1.5°C by implementing strict climate policies immediately to achieve net zero $CO_2$ emissions by 2050. Meanwhile, the \textit{Below 2°C} scenario considers that the previous scenario is somewhat too ambitious, as studies estimate a 67\% probability of limiting the global average temperature increase to below 2°C. 

    The \textit{Low Demand} scenario, in contrast, assumes a global economic shift driven by lower energy consumption rather than aggressive climate policies. It envisions a world where technological advancements, efficiency improvements, and behavioural changes significantly reduce energy demand, thereby curbing greenhouse gas emissions with minimal reliance on carbon pricing or restrictive policies. This scenario implies fewer transition risks due to reduced regulatory pressure but still mitigates physical risks through lower emissions.

    \item\textbf{Disorderly Scenarios} : The key feature of disorderly scenarios is the delay in climate transition. While ultimately aiming to achieve the same objectives as the ordered scenarios, this delay leads to higher transition risks. Even though physical risks remain low, the transition delay results in additional economic costs.

    This scenario, called \textit{Delayed Transition}, assumes that global annual emissions do not decrease before 2030. As a result, strong political incentives are required to successfully limit warming to less than 2°C of global temperature rise.

    % First, the \textit{Divergent Net Zero} scenario, by aiming to achieve net zero emissions by 2050, would generate higher costs due to the introduction of diverging policies across sectors and the rapid elimination of fossil fuels. Moreover, this scenario fundamentally differs from the \textit{Net Zero 2050} scenario because it assumes that climate policies are stricter in sectors that are more energy-intensive and emit more $CO_2$.

    \item \textbf{Global greenhouse scenarios}: Despite the implementation of climate policies in some countries and regions (whether current or planned), scenarios classified as \textit{Hot House World} assume that global efforts alone are insufficient to prevent significant climate warming. Consequently, the global average temperature will reach critical and alarming thresholds, leading to severe physical risks.
    Two scenarios belong to this category: \textit{National Determined Contributions} and \textit{Current Policies}. 

    The first scenario assumes that all climate policies planned by policymakers are considered, even if they have not yet been implemented. This scenario assumes that emissions decrease but still lead to a warming of 2.6°C by 2080, corresponding to progressively severe physical risks and relatively low transition risks. 

    The \textit{Current Policies} scenario, which is much more pessimistic than the first, assumes that only current policies are maintained, inevitably leading to more severe physical risks. The NGFS predicts that under this scenario, $CO_2$ emissions will continue to increase until 2080, resulting in an estimated global warming of approximately 3°C.

    \item \textbf{Too little, too late} : No specific scenario has been developed by NGFS experts for the "\textit{Too little, too late}" category till then. Recently, a new scenario were introduced "\textit{fragmented world}". Fragmented World assumes a delayed and divergent climate policy response among countries globally, leading to high physical and transition risks. Countries with net zero targets achieve these only partially (80\% of the target), while the other countries follow current policies.
\end{itemize}

In summary, these scenarios help various stakeholders in the banking system assess the potential impacts of climate change on the economy and the financial system. In this study, we use the climate scenarios \textit{Below 2°C}, \textit{Delayed Transition}, \textit{Net Zero 2050}, and \textit{National Determined Contributions}. Their selection is justified as they represent each category of climate trends and are widely used in the literature (see \textcite{alogoskoufis2021ecb}, \textcite{garnier2021climate}).\\

For the purpose of the climate stress test on the LGD, we will use the model NGFS NiGEM and the same shocked macroeconomics variables as presented in the previous part. Thus, each NGFS scenario is simulated from 2022 to 20250 using an aggregation of three NiGEMs: GCAM 5.3, MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM, and REMIND-MAgPIE, leading to 15 different projections(3 IAMs × 5 NGFS scenarios) (see figures \ref{fig:cgam}, \ref{fig:message}, \ref{fig:remind} in the appendices).

\chapter{Integration of climate risk in the LGD using the Top-Down approach}

As mentioned in the literature review, the ECB's 2021 climate stress-testing exercise identifies two transmission channels that can be grouped into two main parts to incorporate the impact of climatic factors: the top-down approach and the bottom-up approach. In this section, we focus on the top-down approach for climate stress testing. The other approach will be presented in the chapter \ref{bottom-up_chapter}.\\

The Top-Down approach is based on the hypothesis that climate risk can have a structural effect on macroeconomic variables (gross domestic product, unemployment rate, inflation, etc.), causing systemic stress that can explain banks' financial losses. This approach is summarized in the following diagram :

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    \caption{Overview of the top-down approach}

In the following sections, we will begin by presenting the theoretical concepts of the top-down approach. This will be followed by the implementation of the approach and the presentation of the results.


\section{Theoretical framework: concepts and models}
\subsection{Reference model :  Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF)}

To integrate climate risk into LGD modelling through the Top-Down approach, a common method is to assess the impact of the deterioration of economic conditions caused by climate change on LGD. To do so, the Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF) model of \textcite{vasicek2002distribution}, based of the firm model proposed by Merton, serves as the reference framework for modelling the relationship between macroeconomic conditions (captured through the systemic factor Z) and Point-in-Time (PIT) PDs.


\subsubsection{Merton firm model}\label{merton-presentation}

In the Merton firm model, the debt of a company $i$ at time $t$, denote $D^i_t$, has a fixed maturity period, represented by $H$. Until the maturity (or horizon) $H$, no payments are required, but at that moment, the entire debt must be repaid, i.e. $D^i_t = D^i_H = D^i$ for all $t \in [0;H]$.
The company also possesses assets whose valuation at time $t=0$ is given by $A^i_0$, that will generate wealth that can be used to repay the debt at maturity $H$.
The term $A^i_t$ represents the market value (discounted at time $t$) of the future cash flows generated by the company's activity. This market value depends both on the company's intrinsic competence and on the macroeconomic environment.

At maturity $H$, the owner of the company can assess the value of the assets and has two options. First, if the valuation of the assets is higher than the company’s debt ($A^i_H > D^i$), they can sell the assets to repay the bank and retain the surplus $A^i_H - D^i$. However, if the asset value is lower than the debt, the best choice for the owner is to default and let the bank sell the assets to recover as much as possible. Consequently, in this model, default is defined as the event where the company’s asset value at maturity $H$ is lower than its debt, i.e. $A^i_H < D^i_H$\footnote{In fact, both the company's equity and debt can be interpreted as \textbf{derivative products} on the firm's assets (underlying). It is then possible to use the \textbf{Black-Scholes model} to determine the market value of the debt.}.

This model implies several assumptions: when a company defaults, it does so on its entire debt immediately; The liabilities of the company is independent of its value.

Under these assumptions, and assuming that the value of assets $A^i_t$ follows a Wiener process $b_t$ with drift $\mu_i$ and variance $\sigma_i^2$. We can, hence, define the firm’s value as :

\begin{equation}
\forall t \in [0,T], \quad dA_t^i = \mu_i A_t^i dt + \sigma_i^2 A_t^i db^i_t.
\end{equation}

Thus, using Itō’s lemma, the firm's value at the maturity of its debt can be rewritten as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq1}
    A_H^i = A_0^i e^{\sigma_i \sqrt{H} X_H^i + \left(\mu_i - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_i^2\right)H},
\end{equation}
where $X_H^i = \frac{{b}_H^i}{\sqrt{H}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

To simplify the notation, we assume that the maturity corresponds to one year ($H=1$), so that $A_H^i = A^i$.

Although Merton's model was initially developed to assess corporate default risk, it can also be applied to individuals taking out loans. For an individual borrower, the model's logic can be reformulated by replacing the company's asset value with the borrower’s repayment capacity. This capacity depends on income and wealth (analogous to a firm's assets) and on the level of indebtedness (analogous to liabilities).

However, the formulation given by equation \ref{eq1} is insufficient, because, with these assumptions alone, the Point-in-Time (PIT) probability of default of a company is determined only by the mean growth of its value $(\mu_i)$ and the volatility of this growth $(\sigma_i^2)$. In order to introduce a term reflecting the macroeconomic environment, \textcite{vasicek2002distribution} proposed the Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF) model presented in the following section.

\subsubsection{The Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF) model}

The ASRF model is based on the assumption that the creditworthiness of a borrower $i$ at time $t$, denoted $X_t^i$, depends on a common systemic factor for all borrowers ($Z_t$) and an idiosyncratic factor specific to each borrower ($\epsilon_t^i$):

$$
X_t^i = \sqrt{\rho_i} Z_t + \sqrt{1 - \rho_i} \epsilon_t^i,
$$

where $Z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $\epsilon_t^i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, which implies that: $X_t^i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and the coefficient $\rho_i = \text{corr}(X_t^i, Z_t) = \text{corr}(X_t^i, X_t^j)$ for $i \neq j$, measures the sensitivity of borrower $i$ to the systemic risk.

Default occurs when $X_t^i$ falls below a critical threshold $B_i$, called the default barrier, which is linked to the PIT PD as follows:

\begin{equation}
PD_{PIT}^i(z_t) = \mathbb{P}(X_t^i < B_i \mid Z_t = z_t) = \Phi\left(\frac{B_i - \sqrt{\rho_i} z_t}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right)
\end{equation}

where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

By definition, the Through The Cycle (TTC) PD is given by:

$$
PD_{TTC}^i = \mathbb{P}(X_t^i < B_i) = \Phi(B_i)  \Leftrightarrow  B_i = \Phi^{-1}(PD_{TTC}^i)
$$

We can then express the PIT PD directly as a function of the TTC PD:

$$
PD_{PIT}^i(z_t) = \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD_{TTC}^i) - \sqrt{\rho_i} z_t}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right)
$$



The systemic factor $Z_t$, which reflects the economic conditions, is then extracted as follows:

$$
Z_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}} \left[ \Phi^{-1}\left(PD_{TTC}\right) - \Phi^{-1}\left(\hat{PD}^{PIT}(t)\right) \sqrt{1 - \rho} \right],
$$

where $\hat{PD}^{PIT}(t)$ corresponds to the observed PIT default probability at time $t$, and $\rho$ is set to 15\% in our mortgage loan portfolio in accordance with the regulatory requirements of the Basel II IRB approach.

To assess the impact of macroeconomic conditions on the economic environment several regression models can be used. A decrease in $Z_t$ indicates a deterioration of economic conditions meaning the economy is moving into a more adverse state, which increases the probability of default (PIT PD). Conversely, an increase in $Z_t$ reflects an improvement in economic conditions, reducing the likelihood of defaults.

The relationship between $Z_t$ and macroeconomic variables can be estimated using supervised learning methods (such as time series regression, linear regression, etc.). This allows translating a climate-induced macroeconomic shock — for example, one derived from NGFS scenarios — into a stress on $Z_t$ which will ultimately affect the PIT PD and, indirectly, the LGD through the PD-LGD dependency models discussed earlier.

For the purpose of our study, we assume that the observed PiT PD is the quarterly mean of the credit risk score and the realized LGD, respectively.



\subsection{Modeling the systemic factor: regression approaches and model selection}

\subsubsection{Regression approaches}

To model the systemic factor, it is common to 
use explainable models. Hence, we will focus on linear regression and the Bayesian Averaging of Classical Estimates (BACE) to interpret the results. However, we will also use 'black-box' models such as SVR to challenge these models.

\paragraph{Linear regression}\label{linear_reg}

Linear regression is one of the most fundamental statistical methods for modelling the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It assumes that the target variable can be expressed as a linear combination of explanatory variables, with an error term.
It relies on six main assumptions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Linearity: There is a linear relationship between the explanatory variables and the dependent variable. Hence, the general equation of linear regression is:
    \begin{equation}
    Y = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_j + \varepsilon,
    \end{equation}
    where $Y$ represents the dependent variable, $(X_j)_{j=1,\dots,p}$ are the explanatory variables, $(\beta_j)_{j=1,\dots,p}$ are the coefficients to be estimated using the ordinary least squares (OLS) method, which minimizes the sum of squared errors, and $\varepsilon$ is the error term.
    
    \item Random sample iid from the population: The observed $N$ observations $(Y_i, X_i)_{i=1,\dots,N}$ are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (iid).
    
    \item Full rank: There is no perfect multicollinearity between the regressors. To check this, we use the variance inflation factor (VIF), which measures the degree to which the variance of a regression coefficient is inflated due to collinearity with other predictors. A high VIF indicates strong multicollinearity, typically suggesting that a variable is highly correlated with others in the model. As a general rule, a VIF above 10 is considered problematic.
    
    \item Identifiability: To avoid bias from omitted variables, the error term must satisfy $E(\varepsilon_i | X_i)=0$. This condition, known as the exogeneity assumption, ensures that the regressors $X_i$ are not correlated with the error term.
    
    \item Homoscedasticity and no autocorrelation: The error terms should have constant variance (homoscedasticity), i.e., $V(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$, and should not be correlated, $\text{cov}(\varepsilon_i,\varepsilon_j)=0$ for all $i \neq j$. To detect heteroscedasticity, the Breusch-Pagan test is commonly used with homoscedasticity of the error term as null hypothesis. The Durbin-Watson test is widely used to detect first-order autocorrelation in regression residuals. This test is important since autocorrelated errors can lead to inefficient estimates and unreliable inference, particularly in time series, leading to spurious regression (see \textcite{granger1974spurious}).
    
    \item Normality: The error terms should follow a normal distribution, particularly to ensure valid inference in small samples. The Jarque-Bera and Shapiro-Wilk tests are commonly used to assess normality. In both cases, the null hypothesis is that the residuals are normally distributed.
\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{Bayesian Averaging of Classical Estimates (BACE)}


The Bayesian Averaging of Classical Estimates (BACE) is a Bayesian model averaging technique designed to incorporate parameter uncertainty into regression modelling. Unlike traditional approaches that select a single model, BACE constructs multiple candidate linear regression models and computes a weighted combination of their valid estimates, i.e., the models that satisfy assumptions (2), (5), and (6) of linear regression, including stationarity of the residuals, based on their posterior probabilities. This method is quite relevant in our case, given the relatively short length of your PD series (N=26 points). With so few data points and, therefore, limited information, it is quite difficult to distinguish one model from another and to rely on frequentist selection methods such as stepwise regression or the AIC criterion.

The posterior probability of each model $M_j$ given the observed data $y$ is computed as follows (\textcite{mauri2023lgd}):
\begin{equation}
    P(M_j | y) = \frac{P(M_j) T^{-\frac{k_j}{2}} RSS_j^{-\frac{T}{2}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M} P(M_i) T^{-\frac{k_i}{2}} RSS_i^{-\frac{T}{2}}},
\end{equation}
where $RSS_j$ is the residual sum of squares for model $M_j$, $k_j$ is the number of regressors, and $T$ is the number of observations. The prior probability $P(M_j)$ is typically set to be uniform across all models.
\par
After computing posterior model probabilities, BACE estimates regression coefficients by averaging across models weighted by their posterior probabilities:
\begin{equation}
    E(\beta_i | y) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} P(M_j | y) \hat{\beta}_{i,j},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\beta}_{i,j}$ represents the estimate of the $i$-th variable in valid model $M_j$. This approach ensures robustness by incorporating uncertainty in model selection.
\par
Additionally, BACE calculates the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) for each independent variable, which represents the probability that the variable should be included in the final model:
\begin{equation}
    P(\beta_i | y) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} P(M_j | y) 1_{M_j}(\beta_i).
\end{equation}

A higher PIP indicates that the variable is more relevant in explaining the dependent variable.

\subsubsection{Model selection}

In order to choose the best model, we measure the prediction error of the models using the mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). The model with the best performance based on these metrics will be selected.

These metrics are justified as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): It calculates the square root of average squared differences between predicted and actual values. Squaring the errors penalizes larger deviations more than smaller ones, making it useful for models where large errors are particularly undesirable. The square root of the mean squared error provides error measurements in the same unit as the dependent variable, making it more interpretable. It is given by:
    \begin{equation*}
    RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2},
    \end{equation*}
    where $y_i$ is the actual value, $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted value, and $N$ is the number of observations.

    \item Mean Absolute Error (MAE): It measures the average absolute differences between predicted and actual values, treating all errors equally and being less sensitive to large errors compared to MSE. It is given by:
      \begin{equation*}
      MAE = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
      \end{equation*}
    
    \item Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): It measures the average percentage deviation of predicted values from actual values. This metric is useful for understanding the relative error in percentage terms, making it more interpretable across different scales. It is defined as:
      \begin{equation*}
      MAPE = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i} \right| \times 100
      \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}

Since overfitting is somewhat inevitable due to the small sample size (N=26), we will implement a rolling prediction approach starting with 15 points to identify the model that exhibits the least overfitting.

\subsection{Selected PD-LGD dependency models}

\subsubsection{The Structural Merton LGD}

The structural Merton LGD model links the LGD to the probability of default (PD) through the firm's asset value dynamics as presented in Section \ref{merton-presentation}. Under this model, we can express the recovery rate as the value of the firm's assets divided by its debt at maturity T. The recovery rate at time T, denoted $RR_T$, given that the company defaults, can be written as : 
$$
RR_T := E[\frac{A_T}{D_T} | A_T < D_T], 
$$ 
retaining the same notation as in Section \ref{merton-presentation}. Using Itô's lemma, we can derive an analytical expression for the recovery rate, from which we obtain the LGD, defined as $1 - RR_T$.

Under the assumption of the Merton firm model, the LGD is expressed as: 

\begin{equation}
LGD_{\theta}(PD) = 1 - \frac{\Phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(PD) - \sigma \sqrt{T}\right)}{PD} \exp\left(-\sigma \sqrt{T} \Phi^{-1}(PD) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 T\right),
\end{equation}

where$\Phi(\cdot)$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution,$\sigma$ is the asset volatility, and$T$ is the time to maturity, typically set to 1 year for credit risk applications.

To calibrate the volatility of the asset value$\sigma$, we minimize the following objective function:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\sigma} := \arg \min_{\sigma} \left(RR_{\theta}\left(PD^{TTC}\right) - RR^{TTC}\right)^2,
\end{equation}

where$RR_{\theta}$ represents the recovery rate predicted by the model, and$RR^{TTC}$ is the observed through-the-cycle recovery rate. This calibration ensures that theoretical recovery rates align with the observed ones, thereby allowing the model accurately captures the relationship between PD and LGD.

\subsubsection{Frye-Jacobs model}

The Frye-Jacobs LGD (FJ-LGD) is a recent model that relates asymptotic (i.e. for an infinite portfolio) DR (i.e., empirical PiT PD) and PiT LGD, under three main assumptions : 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The loss rate and the default rate are assumed to have a comonotonic relation, meaning that those paramters approximativeley take the same quantile in their distributions.
    \item The loss rate and the default rate follow a Vasicek two-parameters distribution
    \item The correlation parameter are shared between the default rate and the loss rate.
\end{itemize}

Under these assumptions, Frye and Jacobs modelled the LGD as a function of TTC PD, TTC LGD and the asset correlation $\rho$. The model is given by the following equation:

$$
LGD = \frac{1}{PD_{PIT}} \times \phi \left(\phi^{-1}(PD_{PIT}) -  \frac{ \phi^{-1}(p_{DR}) -  \phi^{-1}(p_{LR}) }{\sqrt{1 - \rho}} \right),
$$

where $\phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, $PD_{PIT}$ is the probability of default at the PIT (Point-In-Time), $p_{DR}$ is the TTC PD (Through-The-Cycle) at the default rate and $p_{LR}$ is TTC loss rate, i.e. $p_{LR} = PD_{TTC} \times LGD_{TTC}$. The asset correlation $\rho$ is the correlation between the default of the obligor and the default of the portfolio.

The FJ-LGD function thus states that if conditions lead to a high PD, they also result in a high LGD, relative to an initial risk parameter defined using the TTC credit metrics.

It is a recent model that has the advantage of being easy to use, requiring only a few parameters that are already available in banks.


\subsubsection{Hillebrand model}

Hillebrand's model establishes a framework that allows for a more flexible dependency between PD (Probability of Default) and LGD (Loss Given Default) than that suggested by the Frye-Jacobs model. It considers that, in addition to a common influence of factors on both variables, some factors may have an opposite influence (when weights are negative) or may affect only one of the two variables (when weights are zero). %\textcolor{red}{For example, the regional real estate index may influence LGD without affecting the PD of a small export-oriented company.}%A typical example mentioned by Hillebrand is the regional real estate index, which may influence LGD without affecting the PD of a small export-oriented company.

In this regard, within the context of Hillebrand's model, PD and LGD are linked by a stochastic relationship.
In this framework, PD and LGD are each driven by two systematic factors, $Z$ and $Y$ %\textcolor{red}{respectively}, which are assumed to follow a standard normal distribution.

PD(Z) is modelled based on the Merton Model, and we denote G(Y) as the expected LGD given Y. %, \textcolor{red}{i.e. $E(LGD|Z)$}.\\

%\hspace*{20mm} \paragraph*{Modeling G(Z)}
%\textcolor{red}{To model $G(Z),$} 
This approach assumes that applying the probit function \( \Phi^{-1} \) to the data reveals an underlying \textbf{linear structure}. Based on this assumption, the function \( G \) is modeled as follows:


\begin{equation}
G(y) = \Phi(a - by), \quad y \in \mathbb{R}
\label{eq:GZ}
\end{equation}
where $ a, b \in \mathbb{R} $ are parameters.

Furthermore, $ Y $ can be decomposed into two components: $ Z $ and an independent factor $ X $.  
This decomposition is defined as follows:

\[
d := \operatorname{cov}(Y,Z), \quad X := \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - d^2}}(Y - dZ).
\] 

By construction, $ X $ follows a standard normal distribution and is independent of $ Y $.  
This leads to the decomposition:

\begin{equation}
Y = dZ + \sqrt{1 - d^2} X.
\end{equation}

This formulation highlights the flexibility in the dependence structure between LGD and PD.  
The factor $ X $ can thus be interpreted as the aggregated influence of systematic factors on $ \mathbb{E}(\text{LGD} \mid Y) $,  
which remains independent of the systematic influence on $ \text{PD}(Z) $.

From (3.5) and (3.6), we obtain:
\begin{align}
    \Phi^{-1} \big( \mathbb{E}(\text{LGD} \mid Y) \big) &= a - bdZ - b \sqrt{1 - d^2} X \\
    &= a - \frac{bdc}{e} + \frac{bd}{e} \Phi^{-1}(\text{PD}(Z)) + b \sqrt{1 - d^2} X,
\end{align}
%Plugging equation (3.3) into (3.7) yields the linear regression equation:
% \begin{equation}
% \Phi^{-1}(\mathbb{E}(\text{LGD} \mid Z)) = a - b d_c e + b d_e \Phi^{-1}(\text{PD}(Y)) - b \sqrt{1 - d^2} X,
% \end{equation}
where $\Phi^{-1}(\mathbb{E}(\text{LGD} \mid Y))$ is the response variable, $\Phi^{-1}(\text{PD}(Z))$ the predictor variable, and $X$ the residual term. %\textcolor{red}{The parameters $(a, b, c, d, e)$ are involved in the model, with:
%$$
%c = \frac{\Phi^{-1}(\text{PD})}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} \quad \text{and} \quad e = \frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}},
%$$
%being predefined, while $a$, $b$, and $d$ must be calibrated, subject to the constraints $bd > 0$ and $e > 0$.}

%\hspace*{20mm} \subsubsection*{ Model Calibration Issues}

For this regression model, we obtain estimates for the intercept $ a - \frac{bdc}{e} $, the slope $ \frac{bd}{e} $, and the standard deviation $ b \sqrt{1 - d^2} $ of the residuals.  
For known values $ c $ and $ e $, this yields estimates for $ a $, $ b $, and $ d $.  
For unknown values $ c $ and $ e $, we estimate them first, and then obtain plug-in estimates for $ a $, $ b $, and $ d $.

With these estimates, we can estimate the downturn LGD at level $ \alpha \in (0,1) $ from equation (3.5) as follows:

\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}(\text{LGD} \mid Z = z_{\alpha}) \approx \Phi(\hat{a} - \hat{b} \cdot z_{\alpha})
\end{equation}

where $ z_{\alpha} $ is the standard normal $ \alpha $-quantile.\\

Consequently, although the Hillebrand model is promising, it could not be adapted to our data.

\subsubsection{Calibration of systemic factor}

We proceed with the estimation of the systemic factor trajectory using the relationship that links the systemic factor to the point-in-time (PiT) probability of default (PD) in the ASRF model. By assumption, the calibrated systemic factor reflects the state of the economic cycle.

Since default rates for both individual and professional borrowers can serve as indicators of economic and financial distress, figure~\ref{fig:systemic_factor} presents their joint trajectory. Unsurprisingly, the observed dynamics reveal a negative relationship between these rates and the systemic factor.

Overall, the results suggest that the calibrated systemic factor captures fluctuations in the economic cycle and the onset of financial crises. In fact, the period under study corresponds to the aftermath of the subprime crisis. An increase in mortgage defaults led to a decline in the systemic factor, signalling economic distress, before gradually recovering.

In this context, and consistent with stress-testing practices, projecting the systemic factor is justified. By leveraging climate impact data on the macroeconomy provided by the NGFS, we can apply stress to PD estimates in order to forecast the corresponding evolution of LGD.

\subsubsection{Macroeconomic Variables}
In order to estimate the PiT PD and LGD models according to the top-down approach, we required macroeconomic variables. We were provided with a dataset containing eight quarterly macroeconomic indicators spanning the period from 2010 to 2021. This dataset was compiled from various open-access sources, including the World Bank, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, and Yahoo Finance. The time span of these variables was restricted to align with that of the portfolio.

\begin{itemize}
    \item GDP Growth Rate, expressed as a percentage, measures the annual change in a country's Gross Domestic Product (GDP), indicating economic expansion or recession.
    
    \item Inflation Rate reflects the percentage change in the general price level of goods and services, commonly derived from the Consumer Price Index (CPI), and influences purchasing power and monetary policy decisions.
    
    \item House Prices (residential) index, benchmarked at 100 in 2019, illustrates the relative change in residential property prices.
    
    \item Interest Rate significantly impacts borrowing and investment; it represents the cost of credit and the return on savings.
    
    \item Long-Term Interest Rate generally applies to loans exceeding ten years and serves as a key indicator of market expectations regarding future inflation and risk.
    
    \item Effective Exchange Rate (EER) is an index-based measure of a currency's value relative to a basket of foreign currencies, benchmarked at 100 in 2017. A higher value indicates appreciation, while a lower value suggests depreciation.
    
    \item Equity Prices index, also benchmarked at 100 in 2017, tracks stock market performance. In the case of France, the selected index is the CAC 40.
    
    \item Oil Prices, expressed in US dollars per barrel, influence global inflation, production costs, and trade balances, affecting both oil-importing and oil-exporting economies.
\end{itemize}

Before constructing the regression model, a feature engineering step is performed. In particular, to avoid spurious regression, the variables must be stationary (see \textcite{granger1974spurious}). Once stationarity is ensured, the regression model can be estimated. For non-stationary variables, we prefer to use the first difference for those expressed as rates, and the relative change for those expressed in absolute terms, in order to achieve stationarity.

Under the null hypothesis, the Augmented Dicker Fuller (ADF) test statistically validates the presence of unit roots, i.e. the variable follows a random walk and hence is not stationarity. When there is not enough statistical evidence not to reject this hypothesis (the theoretical critical value greater than the calculated test statistic or test probability less than the threshold), we conclude that the variables are stationary. Using the p-value, analysis of table \ref{tab:macro_var} shows, for example, that :

\begin{itemize}
    \item the Z systemic factor, the GDP growth rate are stationary at level, i.e. without any transformation ;  
    \item the long term interest rate is stationary in the first difference;
    \item the oil prices are stationary in relative change.
\end{itemize}

Additionally, since we are dealing with economic variables, we must ensure that the observed relationships are economically coherent. Therefore, the house price index, inflation, long-term interest rate, unemployment rate, and oil prices are expected to have a negative impact on the state of the economic cycle. Conversely, GDP and equity prices should have a positive impact, while the effective exchange rate (EER) remains unconstrained.

It is important to highlight that the Spearman correlation coefficient (see figure \ref{fig:spearman-corr}) was chosen over the Pearson coefficient because the scatter plots of variables with factor $Z$ do not all suggest linear relationships (see figure \ref{fig:scatter-corr-Z-macro} in appendice). Theoretically based on the rank of observations, the Spearman coefficient also has the advantage of being robust in the presence of extreme values.\\

However, the observed inflation rate and equity prices do not follow the expected economic signs. Nevertheless, there may be an explanation. During the early stages of recovery, output begins to grow and inflation may rise as demand for goods and services increases. As long as inflation remains moderate, it is typically regarded as a sign of returning economic strength (see \textcite{blanchard2017macroeconomics}).%Moreover, if consumers choose to spend rather than invest, this may decrease demand for financial assets, potentially affecting equity prices.}

Moreover, these Spearman correlations are not statistically significant due to the short observation period. Hence, they will not be removed from the set of eligible variables for the modelling of the systemic factor.

\subsubsection{Models estimation}
To select the best model for predicting the systemic factor, we focus on the one that exhibits the least overfitting, based on the RMSE and MAE performance metrics presented earlier. In our case, the linear regression model with stepwise variable selection shows the lowest level of overfitting (see Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}).

In the interpretable model category, linear regression—also known as ordinary least squares (OLS)—achieves a relatively high $R^2$ of 0.60. However, the increase in RMSE between the training and test samples (169\%) is worse than that observed in other models. The BACE model performs similarly to the OLS stepwise model in terms of RMSE, but its MAE deteriorates between the training and test sets, indicating that, in absolute terms, it does not generalise well.

The OLS stepwise model improves both RMSE and MAE performance between the training and test sets, suggesting that removing irrelevant variables enhances predictive accuracy. Moreover, it achieves an $R^2$ of 0.52, meaning it explains 52\% of the variability in the data, which is reasonably good in practice. It is also associated with a Durbin-Watson statistic close to 2, indicating that the residuals are not autocorrelated and the regression results are therefore reliable.

The SVR model, which serves as the main challenger, appears to outperform the others in terms of$ R^2$ (0.84), indicating that it captures the largest proportion of variance in the data. However, its ability to generalise is poor, as reflected by a Durbin-Watson statistic of 0.42, suggesting that the residuals are autocorrelated.

While the OLS stepwise model does not offer the highest explanatory power in terms of$ R^2$, it minimises overfitting and delivers the most stable estimates in rolling predictions, making it a suitable choice for systemic risk forecasting.

\subsubsection{Projections of the PiT PD}

Instead of showing the projections of the systemic factor using the OLS stepwise model, it is preferable to present the projections of the Point-in-Time (PiT) Probability of Default (PD) using the ASRF model, which links the systemic factor to the PiT PD. This approach facilitates a better interpretation of the results since the PiT PD is inversely correlated to the systemic factor.

As a result, we observe that the baseline scenario, in which no structural changes occur relative to today, is the one where the portfolio exhibits the lowest probability of default. In fact, inflation emerges as the primary driver of the systemic factor in the OLS stepwise regression model. According to the NiGEM data, at the beginning of 2025, we observe an acceleration in inflation, which has a positive impact on the systemic factor, and consequently, a negative correlation on the probability of default.

Moreover, all the climate risk scenarios, in which policies are implemented to account for climate risk based on historical trends and potential risk amplitudes, lead to an abrupt increase in mortgage portfolio defaults before slightly decreasing. Moreover, the Net Zero 2050 scenario has the most significant impact, as it results in the highest default rates in our portfolio. This is logical since this scenario is known to be the strictest, given that climate policies are enforced to achieve a 1.5°C temperature target by 2050.

At the end of the projected period, i.e., 2050, we observe that the Delayed Transition scenario results in the highest default rate. Before 2030, it is the scenario with the second-lowest default rate. However, in 2030, the probability of default increases abruptly, reaching 25\%. This is because the Delayed Transition scenario assumes that global annual emissions do not decrease before 2030. Consequently, strong political incentives become necessary to successfully limit global warming to less than 2°C, leading to an increase in the default rate of our portfolio.

As we can see from this diagnostic table, one of the key assumptions of linear regression is violated: the homoscedasticity of the residuals. This means that the variance of the residuals is not constant across observations, indicating heteroscedasticity. As a result, the standard errors of the regression coefficients may be biased, leading to inefficient estimates and potentially unreliable statistical inference. In fact, one of the key challenges in modeling LGD with linear regression is the presence of significant exogenous factors that influence its realization. In our dataset, LGD is largely driven by external elements such as debt collection policies, regulatory changes, and macroeconomic conditions, which are beyond the control of the borrowers. As a result, the realized LGD can be expressed as :

\begin{equation}
    y^* = y + \epsilon,
\end{equation}

where $y^*$ represents the observed LGD, $y$ is the theoretical LGD explained by the model, and $\epsilon$ captures the noise component, reflecting the influence of uncontrolled external factors.
Given that $\epsilon$ accounts for a substantial portion of the variability in LGD, the model can only explain a limited fraction of $y^*$. This is reflected in the low $R^2$ value (2.7\%), suggesting that while the selected explanatory variables contribute to the model, a large share of the variance remains unexplained. Consequently, the predictive power of the regression is constrained by this noisiness, which makes it difficult to interpret the coefficients of the model.

This ensemble learning method allows for a more flexible representation of non-linear interactions while potentially reducing variance.

Additionally, GridSearch employs cross-validation during the optimization process, which further mitigates overfitting by evaluating the model's performance on multiple subsets of the training data. This approach ensures that the selected hyperparameters are robust and not tailored to a specific subset of the data.

\section{Integration of physical risk}

A key driver of climate-related risk is physical risk, which stems from the increasing frequency and severity of natural disasters due to climate change. In this report, we focus on the impact of floods on LGD. Specifically, we assess how exposure to flood-prone areas affects the recovery rate by incorporating geospatial flood risk data into our analysis. Properties located in high-risk flood zones are identified using flood hazard maps provided by the European Commission, through the LISFLOOD model.

\subsection{Theorical framework : LISFLOOD model}

The flood maps used in this analysis are derived from the LISFLOOD hydrodynamic model. This model generates flood depth maps associated with riverine floods of varying intensities, corresponding to return periods of 10, 20, 50, 100, and 500 years across European countries, and accessible via the European Commission's website. The flood maps are then used to assess the impact of flooding on assets, with the return periods serving as indicators of flood severity.

The production of flood maps for a specific territory involves four key steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Delineation of watersheds: A watershed, or drainage basin, is a geographical area defined by natural boundaries (ridges) where all water flows to a single outlet. Watersheds are delineated using Digital Elevation Models and GIS software like QGIS.
    \item Estimation of a hydrological model: Hydrological models, or rainfall-runoff models, predict river discharge based on climatic data such as precipitation, temperature, and evapotranspiration. These models convert time series of climatic data into discharge series.
    \item Frequency analysis of river discharges: This step involves modelling the distribution of maximum observed discharges over a long period to estimate extreme events, such as a 100-year flood. The Peaks Over Threshold (POT) approach, based on Extreme Value Theory(EVT), is commonly used for this purpose. The POT approach identifies events where the discharge exceeds a certain threshold and models their occurrence using a Poisson process.
    \item  Implementation of a hydraulic model: Hydraulic models simulate water flow in river systems and floodplains using physical equations, such as the Saint-Venant equations. These models convert discharge data into flood depth maps. The LISFLOOD model uses the CA2D hydraulic model to generate flood maps with a spatial resolution of approximately 90 meters.
\end{enumerate}

Damage functions, which link flood depth to asset damage, are applied to estimate the economic impact of floods. These functions are typically based on historical data and expert assessments. For this analysis, the damage functions recommended by \textcite{huizinga2017global} are used, as they are widely accepted by regulators such as the European Central Bank (ECB).

To estimate flood damage on these assets, that particular damage function is used, which relates the flood depth and the height of the building to the expected level of damage. The damage ratio indicates the percentage of the building's value that will be lost due to flooding. This table provides a linear relationship between flood depth and damage. For a given flood height, we can calculate the expected damage as a percentage of the property’s value. For instance, if the flood height is 1.5 meters and the building is taller than 6 meters, the damage ratio would be 50\% for residential buildings and 45\% for commercial buildings.

The damage function for commercial buildings is slightly adjusted compared to residential ones, as commercial properties may have different structural resilience.

The rules for applying the damage function are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item For buildings taller than 6 meters, the damage function remains unchanged. This is based on the assumption that taller buildings are more resilient, and flooding above 6 meters will cause the same damage regardless of their specific height.

    \item For buildings shorter than 6 meters, the damage function is adjusted to reflect their increased vulnerability to flooding. The flood depths in the damage table are scaled down proportionally to the building's height relative to the reference height of 6 meters. This adjustment ensures that shorter buildings, which are more significantly affected by floodwaters due to their reduced capacity to withstand submersion, are accurately represented in the damage assessment.

    Specifically, the flood depths in the damage table are multiplied by the ratio of the building height to 6 meters. For example, a building that is 2 meters tall will use flood depths equal to one-third of those in the original table. A flood depth of 1 meter in the original table is treated as approximately 0.33 meters for this building. This means that a 1-meter flood will cause 75\% damage for a building of this height, while it would cause only 40\% damage for a 6-meter building.

    \item Finally, the damage ratio is determined by comparing the flood height to the values provided in the damage table, which lists flood heights in meters and corresponding damage ratios as percentages. If the flood height falls between two values in the table, linear interpolation is applied to estimate the damage ratio.
\end{itemize}

This adjustment ensures that the vulnerability of shorter buildings is accurately captured, as even relatively small flood depths can have a significant impact on their structural integrity and functionality. Shorter buildings are more vulnerable to flooding because floodwaters can submerge a larger proportion of their structure.

This help stressed the LTV using flood damage data. The stressed LTV is defined as :  

$$
LTV_i^s = LTV \times \frac{1}{1 - D_i^s},
$$

where $D_i^s$ represents the damage percentage for asset $i$ under scenario $s$, which in our case corresponds to different return periods.

\paragraph*{\underline{Focus on the frequency analysis of river discharges}}
The frequency analysis of river discharges is a critical step in flood risk assessment. The POT method is employed to model extreme flood events. This method involves selecting a threshold $S$ and analyzing the occurrences of discharges that exceed this threshold. The process of exceedance is modelled through a Poisson process, where the number of exceedances in a given time period follows a Poisson distribution. The probability mass function of a homogeneous Poisson process is given by:
$$
P(m_S(t) = k) = \frac{e^{-\mu_S t} (\mu_S t)^k}{k!},
$$

where $\mu_S$ is the intensity parameter representing the expected number of exceedances per unit time. This parameter can be estimated using the maximum likelihood method or the method of moments. Thus, $\hat{\mu}_S = \frac{NV}{NA}$,
where $NV$ is the total number of exceedances observed over $NA$ years of data.

The time between exceedances, denoted $\theta_S$, follows an exponential distribution with the survival function:

\[
S(t) = P(\theta_S > t) = e^{-\mu_S t}.
\]

The return period $T(S)$, which is the average time between exceedances of the threshold $S$, is given by:

\[
T(S) = E(\theta_S) = \frac{1}{\mu_S}.
\]

As the threshold $S$ increases, the return period $T(S)$ also increases, reflecting the rarity of more extreme events. However, increasing the threshold reduces the sample size, leading to less precise estimates of the return period. To address this, a statistical distribution $G(y | S)$ is fitted to the exceedances. Commonly used distributions include the exponential, generalized Pareto, and Gumbel distributions. In the LISFLOOD model, the Gumbel distribution is used to model the annual maximum discharges.

The relationship between the return period $T(y)$ and the discharge $y$ is given by:

\[
G(y | S) = 1 - \frac{1}{\mu_S \times T(y)}.
\]

By solving this equation, the discharge $y$ corresponding to any return period $T$ can be estimated:

\[
y(T) = y_0 + a \ln(T),
\]

where $y_0 = S + a \ln(\mu_S)$ and $a$ is a scale parameter estimated from the data.

Once the discharge values for different return periods are estimated, the next step is to convert these discharges into flood depth maps. This is achieved using hydraulic models, which simulate the flow of water in river systems and floodplains. The LISFLOOD model employs the Saint-Venant equations, a set of partial differential equations derived from the principles of fluid mechanics, to model the propagation of floodwaters.

The objective of this section is to examine the Bottom-Up climate stress-testing methodology applied to transition risk and its impact on mortgage credit Loss Given Default (LGD), using the Loan-To-Value (LTV) ratio as the transmission channel.

Transition risk refers to the economic losses resulting from the transition to a low-carbon economy. The real estate sector is particularly vulnerable to asset depreciation, primarily due to increasingly stringent energy efficiency regulations and rising energy costs.

Residential and commercial buildings account for approximately 9\% of total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the European Union, mainly due to their energy consumption according to \textcite{sopgoui2024modeling}. In response, several policy measures have been introduced to improve energy efficiency. For instance, in France, the 2019 Energy-Climate Law prohibits renting properties with poor energy efficiency ratings (EPCs F and G), directly impacting their market value. 
Assessing the impact of these measures on real estate has therefore become essential for banks in order to quantify potential losses.

\subsection{Theoritical Framework : Valuation models}

% \paragraph{Transition Risk in the Real Estate Sector}
% Transition risk refers to the economic losses resulting from the transition to a low-carbon economy. The real estate sector is particularly vulnerable to asset depreciation, primarily due to increasingly stringent energy efficiency regulations and rising energy costs.

% Residential and commercial buildings account for approximately 9\% of total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the European Union, mainly due to their energy consumption according to \textcite{sopgoui2024modeling}. % \paragraph{Real Estate Valuation Models}

The impact of transition risk on real estate is best understood through valuation models. In fact, Transition risk, driven by regulatory changes and energy efficiency policies, leads to asset devaluation. In the literature, three primary approaches are commonly used to assess the value of real estate assets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sales Comparison Method: The property’s value is estimated based on the sale prices of similar properties in the market.
    \item Income Approach: The valuation is derived from the discounted sum of future rental income, making it comparable to the valuation of financial assets.
    \item Cost Approach\footnote{This method is used only when the first two are not applicable}: The property’s value is estimated based on the cost of reconstructing a similar property.
\end{itemize}

Among these, the sales comparison method is commonly implemented using the hedonic pricing model described by \textcite{rosen1974hedonic}, which represents a property's value as a function of its intrinsic and locational attributes:

\begin{equation*}
    P_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Structure}_{i,t} + \gamma \cdot \text{Neighborhood}_{i,t} + \phi \cdot \text{Distance}_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t},
\end{equation*}

where $P_{i,t}$ is the price of property $i$ at time $t$, \texttt{Structure} represents structural characteristics (e.g., number of rooms, floor area), \texttt{Neighborhood} captures locational factors (e.g., proximity to public transport), and \texttt{Distance} measures distances to key amenities.

The income approach, on the other hand, is based on the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model, which estimates property value based on expected rental income :

\begin{equation*}
    P_{i,t} = \mathbb{E} \left[ R_{i,t} + \sum_{s=1}^{T} \frac{R_{i,t+s}}{(1 + \tau)^s} \middle| F_t \right],
\end{equation*}

where $R_{i,t+s}$ is the expected net rental income and $\tau$ is the discount rate, assumed to be constant over time.

\subsubsection{Modeling the impact of transition risk on property values}
%Transition risk, driven by regulatory changes and energy efficiency policies, leads to asset devaluation.

For assessing the impact of transition risk on real estate , we will use the model of \textcite{ter2021german} based on the assumption that, \textit{"all other things being equal, properties with lower energy efficiency will be sold at a lower price than more energy-efficient homes"}. Indeed, in the long term, these properties are going to cost a lot more, not only in energy costs, but also in possible mandatory renovations in the event of regulatory changes on housing energy expenditure.
The price differential between an energy-inefficient property (classified as $j$, with $j$ being lower than $A$) and an energy-efficient property ($A$) is given by :

\begin{align*}
    P^j_t - P^A_t &= - \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{h=1}^{T} \frac{(EC^j_{t+h} - EC^A_{t+h})}{(1 + r)^h} \middle| F_t \right], \\
    &= - \Delta CE^j . \sum_{h=1}^{T} \frac{\mathbb{E} (P^E_{t+h} | F_t)}{(1 + r)^h}
\end{align*}

where r is the discount factor, $EC^j_{t+h}$ and $EC^A_{t+h}$ are the expected energy costs for properties ranked $j$ and $A$, respectively; $P_t^x$, the estimates property value rated $x$; and $\Delta CE^j = CE^j - CE^A$ captures the difference in energy consumption between properties.

The discount factor $r$ reflects the time preference of economic agents when arbitrating between financing costs and energy savings. It is computed as:

\begin{equation*}
r = \alpha \cdot i + (1 - \alpha) \cdot g
\end{equation*}

where $i$ represents the mortgage rate, $\alpha$ is the proportion of debt financing for the property, and $g$ is the expected return on equity, capturing the opportunity cost of invested capital.
For France, using reasonable estimates $r = 0.8 \times 4\% + 0.2 \times 6\% = 4.4\%$.
\newline

%\paragraph{Energy Price Trajectories and Discounted Depreciation Model}

Moreover, the expected energy costs are assumed to be proportional to the energy price at time $t$, multiplied by a constant factor representing annual energy consumption per square meter:

\begin{equation*}
    EC_{t}^{i} = P^E_{t} \cdot CE^i,
\end{equation*}

where $CE^i$ represents the median energy consumption for a given energy rating $i$ (e.g., EPCs classifications). A table giving the median consumption energy values for each energetic class can be found in Appendices (\ref{tab:DPE_CE}).

Thus, the relative depreciation for a property ranked $j$ under scenario $s^*$ is given by:

\begin{equation*}
    D^j_t(s^*) = \frac{\Delta P^j_t(s^*)}{P^A_t(s^*)} = - \left( 
 \frac{\Delta CE^j}{P^A_t(s^*)} \right) \sum_{h=1}^{T} \frac{P^E_{t+h}(s^*)}{(1 + r)^h}
\end{equation*}

where $P^A_t(s^*)$ represents the average price per square meter of energy-efficient properties, thus those that are ranked A, for the scenario $s^*$. As we are conducting a relative rather than an absolute study, the prices of A-rated properties are calculated directly from the database, on the assumption that for all scenarios, the real estate discount for A-rated is $0$.

\subsubsection{Energy price calculations}
The calculation of energy prices $P_E^t$ relies on the assumption that the cost of energy in residential buildings is primarily determined by fossil fuel prices, namely oil and natural gas. In this context, we follow the methodology of \textcite{ter2021german}, which defines the energy price $P^E_t$ as a weighted average of oil and gas prices, converted to a common unit (euro per kWh). Although this method was designed for residential use in Germany, it remains fairly relevant for France due to the way electricity prices are set within the European Union. The price of energy at time $t$ under scenario $s$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
    P^E_t(s) = \omega_O \cdot P^O_t(s) + \omega_G \cdot P^G_t(s),
\end{equation}

where $P^O_t(s)$ and $P^G_t(s)$ are the prices of oil and natural gas, respectively, under scenario $s$, $\omega_O$ and $\omega_G$ represent the relative contributions of oil and gas to energy prices.
\newline

To ensure price comparability, oil and gas prices are converted from their original unit (dollars per barrel of oil equivalent, BOE) into euros per kWh. This requires two conversions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Currency conversion: The prices are converted from US dollars to euros using the exchange rate $e_t$ at a given time $t$:
    \begin{equation}
        P^O_t(s) = P^O_t(s) \cdot e_t, \quad P^G_t(s) = P^G_t(s) \cdot e_t.
    \end{equation}
    \item Energy unit conversion: The energy content per BOE is assumed to be $\gamma = 1700$ kWh. Thus, prices are expressed in euros per kWh as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde{P}^O_t(s) = \frac{P^O_t(s) \cdot e_t}{\gamma}, \quad \tilde{P}^G_t(s) = \frac{P^G_t(s) \cdot e_t}{\gamma}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Additionally, a carbon tax component is incorporated to reflect the impact of climate policies. The carbon tax $T^{CO_2}_t(s)$ is applied to oil and gas prices based on their respective carbon intensity factors $\kappa_O$ (kgCO$_2$/kWh for oil) and $\kappa_G$ (kgCO$_2$/kWh for gas):

\begin{equation}
    P^O_t(s) = \tilde{P}^O_t(s) + \kappa_O \cdot \frac{T^{CO_2}_t(s) \cdot e_t}{1000}, \quad 
    P^G_t(s) = \tilde{P}^G_t(s) + \kappa_G \cdot \frac{T^{CO_2}_t(s) \cdot e_t}{1000}.
\end{equation}
\newline

For the French market, the weights $\omega_O = 35\%$ and $\omega_G = 65\%$ are applied, reflecting the relative contributions of oil and gas to residential energy costs, similar to how it was applied in Germany for the reasons mentionned above.







\subsubsection{Impact of transition risk on the Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio}

To assess the impact of transition risk on mortgage credit LGD, we adjust the LTV ratio based on projected devaluations:

\begin{equation*}
    \forall j \in \mathscr{N}_{-A}, \quad LTV_{i,j}^{s^*} = LTV_{i,j} \left(\frac{1}{1- D^{j}(s^*)}\right),
\end{equation*}

where $D^j(s^*)$ represents the relative depreciation of properties under scenario $s^*$ in absolute terms. This adjustment directly affects LGD calculations, as LTV is a key risk drivers in loss estimation.

By integrating transition risk into the LTV framework, this methodology provides a robust framework for stress-testing the impact of climate-related risks on mortgage portfolios.

%\chapter{Conclusion}
\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}


This project set out to quantify the impact of climate-related shocks on the Loss Given Default (LGD) of a residential mortgage loan portfolio. To achieve this, we adopted a comprehensive methodological framework, combining two complementary approaches to better understand the complex relationship between climate change and credit risk : the top-down approach and the bottom-up approach.\\

We first implemented an indirect, Top-Down approach, which estimates the effect of climate shocks on LGD through their influence on macroeconomic variables—recognized drivers of credit behavior. Within this framework, several PD-LGD dependency models were explored. The structural Merton model was selected for its relatively strong performance (MAPE < 10\%). When applied to NGFS climate scenarios, through the OLS stepwise model, it indicates that the "Delayed transition" scenario results in the highest increase in LGD, while the "Baseline" scenario leads to more limited changes.

This said, this approach is based on models that rely on relatively strong and simplifying assumptions. In particular, they often impose a comonotonic relationship between PD and LGD, meaning both variables are assumed to respond in the same direction to systematic shocks.
%That said, this approach presents several limitations. On the methodological side, simplifying assumptions—such as log-normal asset value distribution and constant volatility—constrain the model's ability to fully capture LGD variability.
On the empirical side, the short data history and the lack of sufficiently granular information negatively affected model performance. This was particularly the case for the Hillebrand model, which, despite being theoretically promising due to its flexible dependence structure between PD and LGD, proved unusable in practice given the limitations of the dataset.\\

To complement this and evaluate the issue from a different perspective, we explored a Bottom-Up approach to assess the direct effects of climate risks on collateral values and LGD.

Physical risk was modeled using the LISFLOOD hydrological model, with damages converted into stressed Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratios. Results show a moderate increase in LGD as scenario severity rises, though this effect is tempered by already elevated baseline LTVs in the portfolio.

Transition risk was modeled through projected depreciation of energy-inefficient properties, using energy price trajectories and regulatory assumptions. These were reflected in adjusted LTV ratios, which served as inputs to a random forest model. The highest LGD values were observed under the Net Zero 2050 scenario, though overall changes remained moderate.

Overall, results from both approaches were directionally consistent: more adverse climate scenarios are associated with higher expected credit losses. 
However, the magnitude of this effect remained limited in the Bottom-Up framework, primarily due to data availability. Key limitations include the lack of precise geographic and energy efficiency data.\\%, the limited predictive power of the models used—particularly the random forest—and the absence of foreclosure indicators, which prevented the implementation of a more advanced two-stage LGD framework.
%However, the magnitude of this effect remained limited in the Bottom-Up framework due to data and model constraints.

%Key limitations include missing geographic and energy performance data, low predictive power of the Bottom-Up models, and the absence of foreclosure indicators—preventing the use of more advanced modeling techniques.

This methodology has shown superior predictive performance in the literature and would offer a more granular view of the recovery process. However, it could not be applied here due to the lack of data indicating whether a property was actually foreclosed.

\section*{LGD estimation for transition risk with linear regression}

We replace \textit{LTV\_actualisee} variable with the stressed LTV variable from each NGFS scenario and apply the regression model to estimate the LGD for each scenario.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lc}
        \hline
        \textbf{Scenario} & \textbf{Predicted Average LGD} \\
        \hline
        Baseline & 0.2875 \\
        Below 2° C & 0.2883 \\
        Delayed Transition & 0.2884 \\
        Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) & 0.2881 \\
        Net Zero 2050 & 0.2905 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Predicted Average LGD under different NGFS scenarios using Stepwise Regression.}
    \label{tab:lgd_predicted_stepwise}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images//bottom-up/lgdStepwise.png}
    \caption{Predicted Average LGD under different NGFS scenarios using Stepwise Regression.}
    \label{fig:lgd_stepwise}
\end{figure}

We observe that the predicted average LGD values (approximately 0.2885) and the average value of the \textit{Realized\_LGD} (around 0.22) are relatively close.  

The difference between the two can be explained by the model's performance, with a low $ R^2 $ of 2.3\%.  

Moreover, we notice that the predicted LGD increases with the severity of the NGFS scenarios. The highest LGD is observed under the Net Zero 2050 scenario (0.2905), which reflects the economic impact of a rapid transition towards carbon neutrality. This suggests that stricter climate policies and their associated transition risks could lead to an increase in expected credit losses. 
\newline

Nonetheless, as detailed in the corresponding section, the performance of this model must be qualified by its poor interpretability and the low proportion of variance explained (low $R^2$). The main assumptions of Linear Regression are not verified. However, we can note that LGDs evolve in a consistent direction as a function of stressed LTVs: a larger LTV, all other things being equal, induces greater exposure to risk.
